Nuclear Renaissance — The Grid That Didn't Build Itself

AI data centers are consuming power faster than the grid can supply it. Intermittent renewables cannot solve the 24/7 baseload problem. Nuclear is the only answer — and Constellation Energy, Vistra, and the SMR pipeline are the structural investment plays of the decade.

The United States added 30 GW of data center power capacity in 2024–2025 and will add another 40–50 GW by 2028. Grid-scale solar and wind produce electricity intermittently — 25–35% capacity factor. Data centers require 99.999% uptime (five-nines reliability). The only 24/7 carbon-free baseload solution is nuclear. This is a structural supply deficit, not a cyclical problem, and it creates a 10-year demand runway for existing nuclear operators.

Part I — The Power Demand Shock

The AI infrastructure buildout of 2024–2027 is creating a power demand shock that no prior technology transition has caused. A single NVIDIA H100 GPU draws 700 watts. A standard AI training cluster of 10,000 GPUs draws 7 megawatts — more than a small town. A hyperscale AI training campus of 100,000 GPUs draws 70 megawatts. Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta are each building multiple such campuses simultaneously, and they need to be operational 24/7/365.

The grid cannot keep up. US electricity generation has barely grown in 15 years — efficiency gains offset population growth. Suddenly, the fastest-growing electricity demand segment is data centers, and it is growing at 25–30% per year. This is a structural supply shock, not a temporary demand spike.

"The question that the AI narrative conveniently ignores: where does the power come from? A 100,000-GPU training cluster running continuously for one year consumes as much electricity as 60,000 American homes. Solar panels produce electricity 25% of the time. Wind turbines produce electricity 35% of the time. Data centers need power 100% of the time."
— CivilisationOS AI Infrastructure Bubble Analysis, August 2026

Part II — Why Nuclear Wins the Baseload Race

The technology comparison is not complex. Data centers need power that is: (1) available 24/7; (2) predictable in output; (3) carbon-free (sustainability commitments); and (4) deliverable at gigawatt scale within existing grid infrastructure. Nuclear is the only source that meets all four criteria simultaneously.

Part III — The Template: Three Mile Island + Microsoft

The Three Mile Island Unit 1 restart (September 2024) is the defining deal that established the nuclear-data-center paradigm. Constellation Energy agreed to restart the 835 MW reactor — shut since 2019 — under a 20-year Power Purchase Agreement with Microsoft. Price: approximately $100/MWh (roughly 3× average market wholesale price). Microsoft's motivation: 24/7 carbon-free power for its AI data centers in Pennsylvania. Constellation's motivation: guaranteed revenue to justify the $1.6 billion restart investment.

This deal created a template that every other hyperscaler immediately began replicating. Google signed a deal with Kairos Power (advanced fission). Amazon partnered with X-energy. Meta and Oracle are in advanced nuclear PPA negotiations. The nuclear-data-center pairing is the dominant energy structure of the AI era.

Part IV — CEG, VST, and the Phase 2 RELOAD Play

Constellation Energy (CEG) is the largest nuclear fleet operator in the United States — 22 reactors, 21 GW of capacity. This is an irreplaceable asset: building new nuclear takes 15–20 years and costs $15–25 billion per plant. CEG's fleet is already built, licensed, and operating. The data center PPA pipeline represents 10–15 years of contracted cash flow. CEG is a secular compounder with a defensible moat that no competitor can replicate on any reasonable timeline.

Vistra Energy (VST) operates 6.4 GW of nuclear plus the largest battery storage system in the US. The nuclear-plus-storage combination is uniquely positioned to capture both the baseload premium and the peak demand premium. VST's Texas market exposure (ERCOT) adds volatility but also captures grid scarcity pricing events.

Part V — The SMR Pipeline (2030+ Horizon)

Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) are the nuclear industry's bet on factory-built, standardised reactor units (50–300 MW each) that can be deployed faster and cheaper than traditional gigawatt-scale plants. NuScale Power became the first SMR to receive NRC design approval in 2022. However, NuScale's Idaho Falls project was cancelled in 2023 after cost estimates ballooned to $22,000/kW — uncompetitive.

The SMR cost trajectory must fall to $5,000–$8,000/kW to be competitive with existing nuclear. This is achievable through factory standardisation and learning-curve effects — but it requires building the first 10–20 SMRs, which requires government backing or committed offtake agreements. The SMR pipeline is real but 2030+ for commercial scale. For Phase 2 RELOAD, the play is existing nuclear (CEG, VST), not SMR development.

Part VI — Capital Cycle Integration

Nuclear sits at the intersection of three macro themes: AI infrastructure (power demand), energy security (post-Ukraine policy shift toward energy independence), and carbon transition (nuclear as the only carbon-free baseload). All three themes are structurally multi-decade, making nuclear one of the most durable long-term holds in the Phase 2 RELOAD portfolio.

The Phase 2 RELOAD doctrine allocates 15% to nuclear/power. Entry targets at AI correction lows — CEG $120–$160, VST $50–$70. These are not trading targets; they are 5–10 year compounding positions that benefit from every hyperscaler that signs a nuclear PPA and every quarter that AI power demand grows.
